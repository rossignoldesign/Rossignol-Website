Elsie MacGill Trailblazer

Did you know that Canada’s first practicing female engineer designed the Maple Leaf Trainer II? Meet Elsie MacGill, whose life had many remarkable “firsts” like the first woman in North America to earn a master’s degree in aeronautical engineering, the first woman ever to design an airplane and the first woman to chair a UN committee. Check out our timeline below of her accomplishments and join Liz Plank as she shares more about Elsie’s incredible life and why it is fitting that she be celebrated on a new commemorative coin.

Elsie MacGill A brief history

1905

Elizabeth Muriel Gregory “Elsie” MacGill is born in Vancouver, British Columbia on March 27th

1927

First woman to graduate with a Bachelor’s Degree in Engineering (University of Toronto)

1929

Becomes ill with polio, leaving her bedridden and unable to walk. Following a long period of rehabilitation, she is able to walk again with the assistance of two canes
Becomes the first woman in North America to achieve a Master’s Degree in aeronautical engineering (University of Michigan)

1938-1943

Hired as the chief aeronautical engineer at the Canadian Car & Foundry (CanCar), becoming the first woman in the world to design and test a new aircraft, the Maple Leaf Trainer II.
It was also during this time she was given the nickname “Queen of the Hurricanes” for her contribution to retooling the plant, enabling the mass production of over 1,450 Hawker Hurricanes

1955

Publishes My Mother the Judge: A Biography of Helen Gregory MacGill, renewing her advocacy for women’s rights

1962

Elected as National President of the Canadian Federation of Business and Professional Women’s Clubs

1967-70

Commissioner for the Royal Commission on the Status of Women, ensuring equality for women in all aspects of society. Until her passing, Elsie dedicates herself to implementing as many of the 167 recommendations as possible

1980

Elsie MacGill passes away in Cambridge, Massachusetts on November 4th

1992

Inducted into the Canadian Science and Engineering Hall of Fame

Unstoppable Elsie:

The incredible but true story of the first woman in Canada to become a professional engineer

The remarkable Elizabeth “Elsie” MacGill isn’t a household name in Canada yet, but once you learn the incredible story of this aeronautical engineer whose work was vital to the Allied war effort during the Second World War who then went on to champion many of the equal rights women enjoy today, you’ll wonder why she isn’t.

When Canadians find a new $1 commemorative coin in their change honouring the Vancouver-born MacGill, they’ll see her portrait on the backdrop of a sky that highlights some of her historic engineering achievements, but if they dig a little deeper into her story they will learn how she paved the way for not only women, but for everyone in her wake.

They will learn that it wasn’t just her brilliant mind that helped her break down barriers for women, but her unconquerable spirit that proved that with hard work, determination and passion, anything is possible. Those attributes helped her not only to excel in aeronautical engineering, but also to elevate others by championing for equal rights.

But just as graduation was around the corner for her Master’s degree, she was struck by polio which paralysed her below the waist. Doctors told her she may never walk again, but after a long period of rehabilitation she did learn how to walk again, although she had to use canes for the rest of her life.

When she accepted her Master’s degree in Aeronautical Engineering from the University of Michigan in 1929, historians believe she was the first woman in North America, and perhaps the world, to do so.

After pursuing some post-graduate work at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, MacGill returned to Canada in the 1930s to add to her string of firsts. She became the first practising professional woman engineer in Canada then in 1938 became the first woman hired as a chief aeronautical engineer when she joined Canadian Car and Foundry (CanCar).

While at CanCar, she designed and tested a new training aircraft, the Maple Leaf Trainer II, the first ever airplane designed and brought to prototype by a woman.

During World War II, MacGill contributed to the retooling of the plant—enabling the mass production of the Hawker Hurricane. She even engineered adaptations to winterize the plane, which sought to make it suitable for cold-weather conditions. Under her supervision, 1,450+ Hawker Hurricane aircraft were produced.

She became so well known for her engineering work that True American Comics told her story in comic book form and in a 1942 issue they dubbed her
“Queen of the Hurricanes”.

By the end of the war, many Canadians began questioning traditional female roles, something MacGill had done from an early age. It’s no wonder as she came from a long line of women’s rights advocates. Her maternal grandmother and mother were suffragists and her mother, Helen Gregory MacGill, defied the sexist norms of her era by obtaining multiple post-secondary degrees. In 1917, she became the first woman in British Columbia to become a Juvenile Court judge. Those role-models played an important role in MacGill’s life, fueling her desire to excel and advocate for people who were discriminated against.

After the war, MacGill continued her engineering work as a consulting engineer with her own company, and became increasingly active in advancing women’s rights, ranging from equal opportunities to education and paid maternity leave. That work earned her a position on the ground-breaking Royal Commission on the Status of Women in 1967.

“It is possible that the effects of this Commission will reach further than people think,” MacGill was quoted as saying during that time. “When considering the status of women, it is important to realize that for both men and women technology is rapidly changing the existing Canadian patterns of employment, full-time and part-time work – and, leisure, too—and the social and economic values upon which status is based. Insight gained there could drastically change Canada’s social philosophy.”

By the 1980s, many of the commission’s 167 recommendations had been either partially or fully implemented by the federal government, thanks in part to MacGill who dedicated herself in the years following to making sure the report wasn’t filed away and ignored.

MacGill never saw herself as a “woman engineer,” but simply an engineer and was a vocal advocate for equality in the workplace.

“It is only habit, custom and complacency that keep us chained to outworn ideas and outmoded institutions. Once we recognize the desirability of change, we can readily find the way to accomplish it,” she once said.

The new 2023 $1 coin honouring her legacy is actually the first commemorative circulation coin that will feature a woman from the post-Confederation era that isn’t an athlete.

MacGill’s career earned her a long list of accolades, including the Award for Meritorious Contribution to Engineering from the Society of Women Engineers, the Association of Professional Engineers of Ontario’s Gold Medal, the Amelia Earhart Medal from the International Association of Women Pilots and she was named as an Officer to the Order of Canada. After her death in 1980, she was inducted into both the Canada’s Aviation Hall of Fame and the Canadian Science and Engineering Hall of Fame.

While her fame transformed her into a role model that inspired other women to follow in her footsteps to pursue careers in science, technology, engineering and math, MacGill thought her efforts to fight for equal rights were equally important.

“To all but the faint-hearted among us, responsibilities are a welcome call on our ability and energy. Our individual responsibilities supply the zest and joy of living; our family responsibilities, responsibilities to our friends, our work, our club, our community, nation and the world at large – life without these would be worth little to any of us.”

The next time you find an Elsie MacGill coin in your change, think about the story of this feminist role model who cemented herself into Canadian history by using her ambition to change the status quo, then ask yourself what can you do to be more like MacGill to make this country a better place?
