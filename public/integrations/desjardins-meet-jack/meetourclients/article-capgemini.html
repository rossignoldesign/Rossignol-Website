“Back to normal isn’t our focus”

- How Capgemini is building a new, better vision of employee support.
4

For Colm Sparks-Austin, President and Managing Director of Capgemini Canada, employee wellness is “ten miles wide and one-hundred miles deep.” As Covid-19 pushed employees into different work habits—everything from home working to fully digital interfacing—it was clear that employees expected new flexibility and wellness practices to be embraced as they emerged from the pandemic. When looking for a health and benefits partner aligned with their vision and values, Capgemini found Desjardins Insurance’s approach to holistic wellness was a major differentiator. Over a seven-year partnership, Desjardins Insurance has been a collaborative partner in helping to shape Capgemini’s wellness programs, providing guidance and expertise, to help Capgemini not only introduce those new programs, but also facilitate workshops and clinics for employees.

“Back to normal isn’t our focus,” says Sparks-Austin. “For many people that ‘old normal’ wasn’t working. During the pandemic, we got used to a hybrid lifestyle, and a lot of people don’t want to go back to the way things were. Now we’ve been working on getting our managers discussing with employees about how work is going and how their mental state is.”

Questions around happiness can be very subjective, and the answers may have nothing to do with employees’ work-life balance.

With over 2000 people working across Canada, it wasn’t an easy task, especially in the wake of digital fatigue. The lack of in-person contact caused by Covid didn’t allow people to properly communicate how they were feeling, and solely interacting online felt impersonal. To avoid creating programs that were overly general, Sparks-Austin says something more nuanced and personal was needed: “When we looked at mental health, we realized we needed to make a lot of changes around traditional benefits that divided people into either ‘happy’ or ‘sick’. There was nothing in-between.” Modernized, expanded insurance programs, like ones offered by Desjardins Insurance, are designed to incorporate these more nuanced views of mental health.

Questions around happiness can be very subjective, and the answers may have nothing to do with employees’ work-life balance. Instead, Sparks-Austin says that at Capgemini, managers are encouraged to ask more direct questions about their enthusiasm for the role and ambitions to grow long-term within the company that get to the heart of someone’s feelings about work. To help keep the answer to those questions positive, Capgemini invested in three pillars of employee wellness—benefits, work-life balance, and a hybrid work model.

Feeling like a part of something bigger is key to providing one’s full worth. Rather than having commands issued from on high, Sparks-Austin wants to ensure there is two-way communication across the company. In what Sparks-Austin calls “aligned entrepreneurship,” employees are encouraged to do their job in a way that suits their particular skill, allowing them to feel that they are valued for their singularity, not simply as a body.

“We use a two-way dialogue that not only hasn’t existed in Capgemini, but across the industry.”

“Technology won’t overtake human relationships, this is about humans.”

Sparks-Austin says it was pivotal to understand people’s shifting attitudes toward hybrid work, particularly the way working from home created a need to juggle family and social responsibilities with work obligations. At Capgemini, there wasn’t only strong two-way communication between employees and managers to find the best solution based on each person’s role and any client requirements, but also an emphasis on asking the right questions about the employee’s preferred lifestyle and matching that to business requirements. For those working from home on more of a permanent basis, Capgemini provided the means to invest in better home offices, from computer monitors to ergonomic chairs. Capgemini’s people managers are also evolving and practicing new collaboration techniques for connecting teams together in a hybrid work environment. Part of this is also geared to creating a purpose for our teams to engage with each other that extends beyond just their work.

Desjardins Insurance’s flexibility has allowed Capgemini to provide their employees with a wide array of program choices, and to educate team members on the health improvements important to Capgemini, such as weight management and mental health coverage. To that end, Capgemini have also rolled out several programs to encourage physical fitness, including online yoga sessions, and access to a fitness app. Employees also have access to open, “ask me anything” style forums, and a “Capgemini Speaks” hotline, which they can call to discuss whatever they like regarding the company and their work. The latest benefit is a virtual doctor service available for employees and their families.

All these are what Sparks-Austin considers “thoughtful” benefits, rather than “extra” benefits—encouraging employees to have a clear line of communication with decision-makers, and maintain the two-way relationship between manager and employees Capgemini seeks.

“Technology won’t overtake human relationships,” Sparks-Austin says. “This is about humans. Trying to design policies without considering who they affect has been our biggest learning. Bringing in happiness and fun is a constant dialogue.”
