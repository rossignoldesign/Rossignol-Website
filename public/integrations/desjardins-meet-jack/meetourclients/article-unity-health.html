How Unity Health Toronto brought together empathy and business,

to give employees a benefit plan that they actually want
1

If we weren’t aware of the stress involved in health care previous to 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic has opened our collective eyes to the pressure and strain of working in the medical profession. In hospitals across the country, mental health has become the “elephant” in the room. Barry Lam, manager for safety and wellness at Unity Health Toronto, says the pandemic kicked off the need for leadership commitment to mental health wellness, something that was often missing in healthcare’s faced-paced environment. “Obviously, a hospital is quite busy,” Lam says, “and we struggled to find time to talk about wellness.” Having confidence that their insurance provider is both sympathetic and flexible enough to handle the fast-changing world of health care is a top priority for Unity Health. With Desjardins Insurance behind them, there was no question that Unity Health was confident in committing to mental health initiatives.

Unity Health works across Toronto, with St. Joseph’s Health Centre, St. Michael’s Hospital and Providence Healthcare under their purview. With so many locations and staff to look after, it was obvious the participation of staff would be vital.

“We needed to know what staff wanted to do to relieve stress”, says Cheryl Croutch, Unity Health’s director of corporate health, safety, and wellness. “Without their input, there’s no point in having the programs.”

After an engagement survey, where hospital staff indicated that mental health and wellness was one of their priorities, Unity Health instituted their “joy in work” strategic priority—a program wherein programs and initiatives are designed collaboratively between managers and employees. Upholding the wellness framework are six pillars of wellness: social, intellectual, occupational, physical, emotional, and spiritual. While Lam acknowledges that, across their hospitals, there is a wide array of different needs and desires regarding mental health—from clinical to support services, from those with physically active jobs to those in more sedentary roles—these pillars are universal.

Playing on the various coloured “emergency codes” used for a variety of medical situations in the hospital setting, Unity Health instituted “Code Lavender” as the call to allow staff to summon onsite mental health resources. Furthermore, Unity Health has brought in mental health first aid training, and, to keep up morale, there are journaling sessions, open mics, group walks, and games. Weekly themed days—Mindful Mondays, Tasty Tuesdays, Fitness Fridays—give form to workdays that otherwise run together under a heavy workload.

Benefits also extend away from work, and into the home, with employees receiving Foundation funded “wellness bags” that contained snacks, garden seeds, exercise equipment, and mental health resources, and a $100 voucher for wellness activities.

“It’s not often that employers provide such a specific gift of wellness for their staff,” Croutch says.

The Joy Fund is another initiative that is providing up to ten thousand dollars to support activities that will bring joy to the work team. Employees suggest their own activities, are encouraged to think outside the box, and successful applications have seen Unity Health bringing in an artist for a drawing session, and a network-wide talent show that had staff and physicians and teams from across the sites join in and tune in to cheer their colleagues on.

Many of these programs, now in their second year, create the sense of community that was lost in the isolation and uncertainty of the pandemic, and have gone a long way to creating a unified workforce.

Thanks to adaptive Desjardins Insurance, there is room to adapt coverage to whatever employees deem necessary in their fluid work environment. “There’s always lots of room to grow,” Croutch says. “We’re looking at launching wellness centres across our sites, which could have fitness centres and dedicated space for wellness activities. We want to make sure we get right down to the front-line staff. It’s not just doctors and nurses who need mental health care, it’s for everyone.”

“We just created a standard evaluation tool to measure the impact these sessions have on employees’ day-to-day life.” One initiative, the March Madness Wellness Challenge, encourages employees to take time every day for their wellness, with participants winning prizes along the way. “Tasks as simple as going sugar free for the day, TV detox day or creating art. Life gets busy and hectic, but I think it’s so important to take a little bit of time for ourselves every single day,” said one participant who discovered different ways to work on their own wellness thanks to the program.

Another participant said “the wellness challenge has taught me that in an ever-changing world it is good to take time out to do the things that rejuvenate you whether it be physical, mental, spiritual, and emotional. You are not only doing things to help alleviate stress but you are also doing yourself a favour by keeping healthy, keep moving, motivating yourself, taking time out for yourself or with co-workers, friends and family. I think it is important to incorporate time for wellness even if it is in small increments.”

Croutch says the network’s focus on wellness has been designed in a way that it can be a personal choice in terms of how our staff incorporate it into their daily lives and routines – and the programs empower teams to keep the momentum going.

“Our staff work at other hospitals, and many of them say that those other locations aren’t doing what Unity is doing. But that way, our message spreads to other workplaces,” said Croutch.“If healthcare workers are well themselves, then they will provide the best care for their patients,” Croutch says.
